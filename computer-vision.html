Computer Vision

Introduction

Computer Vision (CV) is a field of Artificial Intelligence that trains computers to interpret and understand the visual world. By using digital images from cameras and videos, AI models can accurately identify and classify objects, and then react to what they "see" [1].

Until recently, CV relied heavily on manual feature engineering. Today, Deep Learning, specifically Convolutional Neural Networks (CNNs), has become the dominant approach, allowing machines to achieve human-level or superhuman accuracy in specific visual tasks.

How it Works

Computer vision works by breaking down images into pixels and analyzing their colors and patterns. A CNN applies filters that scan across the image, detecting simple edges first, then complex shapes like circles, and eventually specific objects like a car or a human face.

Core Concepts and Algorithms

  • Image Classification: Determining what category an image belongs to.
  • Object Detection: Identifying and locating multiple objects within an image using bounding boxes.
  • Image Segmentation: Partitioning an image into multiple segments to understand it at the pixel level.

Brief History

Early experiments in the 1960s aimed to give computers visual input. The field faced a major hurdle known as the "Variation Problem" (objects look different from various angles and lighting). This was largely overcome in the 2010s with the advent of large datasets (ImageNet) and deep CNNs [2].

Real-world Applications

CV is used in facial recognition for smartphones, obstacle detection in self-driving cars, and automated quality inspection in manufacturing plants.

Popular Tools, Libraries, and Models

OpenCV is the standard open-source library. YOLO (You Only Look Once) is famous for real-time object detection, and ResNet is widely used for classification.

Advantages, Challenges, and Limitations

Advantages: Can process visual information faster and more accurately than humans in repetitive tasks.

Challenges: Highly sensitive to lighting and camera angles. Requires vast amounts of annotated data to train effectively.

Future Outlook

The integration of Vision Transformers (ViTs) is pushing the boundaries of CV, moving beyond CNNs. There is also a push toward "zero-shot" learning, where models can recognize objects they have never explicitly been trained on.
